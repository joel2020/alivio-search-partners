Most failed health system searches do not fail at the offer stage. They fail in the first two weeks, quietly, in decisions that felt procedural at the time. By the time the search is visibly stalled — month four, no finalists, a committee losing faith — the cause is usually upstream and structural.

These are the five failure points we see most often.

## The five failure points

**1. The spec is a peace treaty, not a profile.** When the success profile is written to satisfy every stakeholder, it describes a person who does not exist: a physician-executive with 15 years of P&L ownership, deep informatics credentials, and a willingness to relocate for below-market compensation. Committees sign off because nothing in it is objectionable. Candidates decline because all of it together is impossible.

**2. Compensation is benchmarked against the org chart, not the market.** Internal equity is real, but the market does not care about it. A CMO range set 20% below the regional market does not produce a cheaper CMO. It produces a six-month search and then the same market-rate hire, minus six months.

**3. The committee is built for governance, not decision speed.** Eleven voting members with quarterly availability cannot run a 90-day process. Candidates read slow committees as institutional indecision — and they are usually right to.

**4. The real reason the seat is open is undiscussable.** If the last executive left because of the COO, the board knows it, the market knows it, and the candidates will find out in their first backchannel call. A search that cannot name the problem cannot screen for the person who can survive it.

**5. Physicians hear about the search last.** Clinical leadership hires that surprise the medical staff arrive with organized opposition before day one. The medical executive committee does not need a vote. It needs to not be ambushed.

## What the successful searches share

The searches that close in 60–75 days make the opposite five decisions before any candidate is contacted: a falsifiable success profile, market-tested compensation, a decision group of five or fewer, a written and honest account of the vacancy, and clinical stakeholders engaged in the calibration round. None of this is sophisticated. All of it is uncomfortable. That is why it predicts.
